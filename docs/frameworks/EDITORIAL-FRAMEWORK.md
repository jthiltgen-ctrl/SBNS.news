# ShockedButNotSurprised.news Editorial Framework

Status: Proposed canonical editorial constitution
Lineage: EDITORIAL.md, Stories 001–005, ADMIN-INTAKE-SPEC.md, v1.4 fixtures and publication/monitoring contracts

## 1. Mission

ShockedButNotSurprised.news is an accountability publication focused on institutional and systemic conditions worth public scrutiny: failures, persistent underperformance, foreseeable or accepted risk, waste, displaced burdens, inequitable or unjust outcomes, perverse incentives, accountability gaps, and other consequential differences between what systems are expected to do and what documented evidence shows they actually do.

Its voice may be weary, dark, dry, or incredulous. Its factual core must remain sober.

The publication is not an outrage generator. The story is not that something sounds absurd. The story is that a consequential institution, policy, process, control, allocation of resources, incentive, or concentration of power produces or tolerates an accountability condition the evidence can actually support.

A discrete institutional "failure" is one valid accountability condition, not a universal prerequisite for coverage.

The governing editorial sentence is:

Evidence first. Accountability second. Rhetoric last.

## 2. Human editorial authority

Publication is always a human editorial decision.

Automation and AI may:

- retrieve and normalize evidence;
- compare sources;
- identify claims and qualifications;
- surface contradictions;
- classify category and severity;
- recommend PUBLISH, HOLD, or REJECT;
- draft a proposed headline, summary, kicker, tags, and source list;
- validate deterministic publication artifacts.

Automation and AI may not:

- invent a source;
- silently replace a source;
- transform recommendation into publication;
- erase a qualification for cleaner copy;
- turn uncertainty into certainty;
- approve its own draft;
- auto-correct published history without human review.

Recommendation, decision, approval, repository publication, deployment, and live verification are separate events.

## 3. Editorial fit

A candidate belongs in ShockedButNotSurprised.news when the evidence supports meaningful public-accountability relevance.

Ask:

- What institution, system, policy, program, process, control, or exercise of power is at issue?
- What was it supposed to do?
- What actually happened?
- What consequence, risk, cost, right, service, public resource, or accountability interest is implicated?
- What is current or newly documented?
- What is the institutional nexus: design, policy, authority, resource choice, implementation, management, incentive, control, omission, repeated performance pattern, or allocation of burden?
- Does the evidence support a discrete failure, persistent poor performance, foreseeable/accepted risk, waste, burden transfer, inequitable outcome, rights or justice concern, accountability gap, perverse incentive, or another consequential institutional tension?
- Is that accountability condition broader than isolated personal misconduct, or does an isolated event reveal a broader institutional condition?
- Is the story meaningfully different from what has already been published?

Useful accountability lenses include:

- promise versus practice;
- authority versus accountability;
- policy versus implementation;
- design versus lived consequence;
- incentive versus public cost;
- repeated poor results versus continued institutional acceptance;
- known or foreseeable risk versus mitigation;
- waste, duplication, delay, or opportunity cost;
- who benefits versus who bears the burden;
- institutional convenience versus displaced stakeholder burden;
- formal compliance versus operational reality;
- legal sufficiency versus practical justice or equity concerns;
- control design versus control performance;
- public claim versus documentary record;
- concentrated power versus meaningful recourse.

The existence of a bad outcome alone is not enough. But neither is the absence of a single provable malfunction a reason to reject a consequential accountability story.

A system may be newsworthy because it repeatedly performs poorly, predictably produces a burden, tolerates known risk, wastes resources, externalizes costs, creates a documented disparity, or works as designed in a way that itself warrants scrutiny.

## 4. Background before conclusion

Chronology is evidence.

Before a strong conclusion, preserve enough background to know:

- what was known at each stage;
- what was still uncertain;
- what authority or policy existed;
- what actions were taken;
- what alternatives were available;
- what later information changed the picture.

Precedent is not proof. Analogy is not equivalence. Historical pattern is not causal certainty.

Do not flatten a developing record into a conclusion that only became possible later.

## 5. Evidence standard

Every material factual claim must be supportable by identified evidence.

Source authority is claim-specific.

Examples:

- an agency release may be authoritative for what the agency says it did;
- an audit may be authoritative for its own findings and scope;
- a statute may be authoritative for the text of the law;
- independent reporting may establish facts not contained in official records;
- an institution is not automatically an independent verifier of its own performance.

Prefer, where available:

- primary records;
- official reports, audits, court records, statutes, regulations, and data;
- independent reporting;
- the institution's response;
- relevant technical or subject-matter context.

Independent reporting is desirable but not mandatory when authoritative primary records independently establish the material facts.

No source may be implied to have been reviewed if it was not actually reviewed.

## 6. Claim ledger

Material claims should be treated individually.

For each material claim, preserve:

- the claim;
- whether it is verified, verified with qualification, disputed, or unverified;
- the sources supporting it;
- any material limitation;
- any conflict among sources;
- whether the claim is essential to the proposed story.

A proposed story should not be published while a core material claim remains unverified.

A disputed claim may appear only when the dispute itself is accurately represented and editorially relevant.

## 7. The accountability and causation ladder

Do not force every story through the word "failure." Separate these propositions:

1. **Observed condition** — what the record shows happened, exists, recurs, costs, delays, burdens, distributes, or risks.
2. **Institutional nexus** — how a policy, design, authority, decision, omission, incentive, resource allocation, control, implementation choice, or repeated management pattern is connected to that condition.
3. **Accountability characterization** — what the evidence supports calling that relationship: failure, weakness, persistent underperformance, foreseeable or accepted risk, waste, burden transfer, inequitable outcome, accountability gap, perverse incentive, or another bounded characterization.
4. **Specific harm or consequence causation** — whether that institutional condition caused a particular harm or consequence.

These are different propositions. A publishable story does not always require Step 3 to be labeled "failure," and it does not always require proof of Step 4.

Recurring safeguards:

- an institutional nexus does not equal proven specific-harm causation;
- repeated poor performance can be accountability-relevant without proving misconduct;
- a system may warrant scrutiny because foreseeable burdens or risks are accepted as normal;
- a system-wide policy weakness can coexist with limited field-level sampling;
- sensitive communities increase the review burden without determining publishability;
- an observed condition can have multiple causes and must not automatically be attributed to the institution;
- a clean financial-statement opinion can coexist with statutory, compliance, budget, control, waste, or burden-allocation concerns;
- a system functioning as designed can still produce an accountability-worthy documented outcome.

Do not write a stronger institutional relationship than the evidence supports.

Do not convert a documented disparity, burden, or poor result into an assertion of unlawful discrimination, intent, injustice, or causation unless the evidence and applicable framework support that characterization.

When specific-harm causation is not established, say what is established and stop there.

## 8. Qualification is evidence, not clutter

A qualification that materially changes meaning must survive into final copy.

Do not remove qualifications because they:

- weaken a dramatic narrative;
- make a headline less tidy;
- complicate the kicker;
- reduce certainty;
- make institutional responsibility narrower.

If a source gives multiple explanations, the story must not attribute every instance to institutional failure unless evidence supports that attribution.

If evidence is incomplete, say so.

If evidence conflicts, surface the conflict.

If the conflict cannot be resolved, do not invent the missing bridge.

## 9. Technical and legal terms

Interpret technical terms according to their actual scope.

Do not casually convert:

- budget-control failure into fraud;
- questioned cost into theft;
- compliance finding into corruption;
- clean audit into no findings;
- allegation into fact;
- risk into demonstrated harm;
- policy authority into proof of proper implementation.

When a term has a specific legal, accounting, auditing, regulatory, scientific, or programmatic meaning, preserve that meaning.

## 10. Institutional response

A meaningful institutional response should be sought or included when reasonably available.

Record whether the institution:

- agrees;
- disagrees;
- qualifies;
- explains;
- mitigates;
- has already corrected the issue;
- promises corrective action;
- disputes the underlying premise.

Including the response does not require adopting it.

Ignoring a material response because it weakens the story is not acceptable.

## 11. Sensitive subjects

Subjects involving deaths, crime victims, Indigenous communities, children, health, disability, sexual violence, incarceration, addiction, poverty, or other vulnerable populations require higher review of:

- attribution;
- causation;
- language;
- proportionality;
- privacy;
- headline framing;
- humor.

The people living with the consequences are not the punchline.

## 12. Humor and the FML kicker

Humor must punch up.

Acceptable targets include:

- institutions;
- bureaucracy;
- management choices;
- policy contradictions;
- public promises;
- control failures;
- concentrated authority;
- procedural absurdity.

Unacceptable targets include:

- victims;
- vulnerable communities;
- people harmed by the system;
- people whose circumstances are incidental to the institutional failure.

The kicker must remain inside the factual boundaries of the story.

A joke is not permission to make a stronger causal claim than the reporting supports.

When the subject cannot safely support humor, omit or soften the kicker.

## 13. Severity

Severity is editorial judgment, not a source fact.

Use the scale consistently:

- Severity 1: minor administrative or procedural failure with limited demonstrated consequence.
- Severity 2: clear control, compliance, management, or governance failure with limited or moderate demonstrated consequence.
- Severity 3: meaningful systemic failure with substantial operational, financial, rights, service-delivery, or public-accountability consequences.
- Severity 4: serious systemic failure or risk involving public safety, civil rights, large-scale authority, significant public resources, or similarly high stakes.
- Severity 5: extreme institutional failure involving demonstrated or substantial risk of severe harm, widespread direct consequences, or exceptionally serious abuse of public power.

Severity does not automatically rise because:

- a dollar figure is large;
- a topic is national;
- a subject is emotionally charged;
- a headline is shocking.

Record the rationale.

## 14. Category

Current public categories are International, National, and Local.

Classify by the practical geographic and institutional scope of the story, not by where a source happens to be published.

## 15. PUBLISH, HOLD, REJECT

### PUBLISH

Use when evidence supports a current, consequential, institutionally relevant story and material editorial risk is manageable.

Publication requires:

- a meaningful documented public-accountability condition or tension;
- support for all core material claims;
- preserved qualifications;
- acceptable attribution and causation;
- a source set adequate to the claims being made;
- an acceptable kicker target;
- explicit human approval.

### HOLD

HOLD is a successful editorial outcome when a story may become publishable but uncertainty remains.

Typical reasons:

- missing primary documentation;
- insufficient corroboration;
- unresolved source conflict;
- institution response still needed;
- developing facts;
- unclear causation;
- ambiguous jurisdiction or scope;
- duplicate uncertainty;
- factual or legal review needed;
- unsafe or premature humor.

Holding a story is better than forcing certainty.

### REJECT

Use when the candidate does not meet the publication's standards.

Typical reasons:

- no meaningful public-accountability relevance after considering the full accountability aperture;
- isolated misconduct without broader accountability relevance;
- opinion presented as fact;
- unsupported speculation;
- trivial consequence;
- stale material without meaningful new development;
- duplicate without meaningful new information;
- inadequate sourcing;
- unsupported allegation;
- misleading causal framing;
- incurable factual or legal risk;
- humor would primarily punch down.

REJECT is not a failure of the system. It is part of the system.

## 16. Editorial aperture and anti-throttling control

Editorial rigor must not collapse into a single-keyword gate.

Before rejecting, stopping, or withholding a candidate primarily because a discrete institutional "failure" is not established, check whether the evidence instead supports another SBNS accountability frame, including:

- persistent or repeated poor performance;
- an expected, foreseeable, or previously identified failure mode;
- institutional tolerance of known risk;
- waste, duplication, delay, or avoidable public cost;
- displacement of time, money, risk, administrative burden, or responsibility onto residents, workers, patients, customers, taxpayers, local governments, or other stakeholders;
- inequitable distribution of benefits, burdens, access, service, or recourse;
- a documented rights, fairness, or justice concern;
- a perverse or contradictory incentive;
- formal compliance paired with materially poor real-world performance;
- power without proportionate transparency, accountability, or recourse;
- a system functioning as designed where the design itself produces a consequential documented problem;
- normalization of a condition that would otherwise warrant public explanation.

This is an **aperture check**, not a lower evidence threshold.

Do not manufacture a frame merely to keep a candidate alive. The record must support the accountability characterization actually used.

Likewise, do not let repeated REJECT, HOLD, or NO ACTION outcomes pressure the system toward either easier publication or narrower screening. Monitor disposition patterns for possible semantic or gate bias, then inspect representative cases.

A high rejection rate may mean the discovery stream is noisy. It may also mean the gate is overfitted to one conception of institutional failure. The rate alone proves neither.

### Justice, equity, and burden language

Words such as `unjust`, `inequitable`, `discriminatory`, or `unfair` can carry legal, ethical, empirical, or normative meanings.

When using them:

- identify the factual distribution or burden first;
- distinguish measured disparity from inferred motive;
- distinguish legal violation from ethical judgment;
- identify the standard, right, promise, comparator, or principle being used when material;
- attribute contested normative characterizations rather than presenting them as source facts.

SBNS may report and analyze documented burdens and disparities without requiring proof of malicious intent.

## 17. Research stoppage

Research is not valuable merely because more of it is possible.

Stop or defer when:

- the material facts needed for a responsible decision are established;
- additional searching is producing repetition rather than new evidence;
- remaining uncertainty is immaterial to the claim actually being made;
- the candidate has reached a legitimate HOLD or REJECT state;
- a future source or event is the real dependency.

Do not chase a preferred conclusion.

Do not keep searching solely to find support for a narrative the existing record does not sustain.

“No action required” is a valid state.

## 18. Drafting rules

A story should make the strongest claim the evidence supports, not the strongest claim language can carry.

A good ShockedButNotSurprised.news draft should:

- identify the relevant institution, system, or concentration of power quickly;
- state the documented accountability condition precisely;
- include scale and consequence when supported;
- preserve material context and response;
- distinguish fact from editorial severity and tone;
- use a kicker that targets power rather than harm;
- cite real sources that readers can inspect.

## 19. Publication lifecycle

The editorial lifecycle is:

Discovery or watch
→ Intake
→ Evidence gathering
→ Claim and causation analysis
→ PUBLISH / HOLD / REJECT recommendation
→ Human review
→ Human-edited exact draft
→ Human decision
→ Deterministic publication preparation
→ Repository review
→ Authorized deployment
→ Live verification
→ Monitoring
→ Correction, update, follow-up, or no action

No arrow may be assumed merely because the prior step succeeded.

## 20. Corrections, updates, and follow-ups

Never silently rewrite published history.

Use:

- correction when the original published reporting was materially wrong;
- update when later information changes the present situation but the original reporting was accurate;
- follow-up when a later development deserves a distinct story;
- no action when the development is immaterial.

Corrections should preserve:

- the earlier version;
- the corrected version;
- what changed;
- why;
- supporting evidence;
- the deciding editor;
- relevant timestamps.

## 21. Political and ideological neutrality

Institutional accountability is not partisan loyalty.

Apply the same evidence and causation standards to governments, parties, administrations, agencies, officials, contractors, corporations, nonprofits, and other centers of power.

Do not use the publication to tell readers how to vote.

When political actors are involved, describe documented actions, policies, records, consequences, and disputes rather than converting editorial accountability into political endorsement.

## 22. Provenance and humility

For consequential conclusions:

- distinguish documented fact from editorial inference;
- identify uncertainty;
- preserve contrary evidence when material;
- avoid claiming novelty merely because a connection feels new;
- invite correction when the record can be improved.

The publication's credibility depends less on sounding certain than on showing why a claim deserves confidence.
